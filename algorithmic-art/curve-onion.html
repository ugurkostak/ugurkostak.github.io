Curve Onion

May 2026

Plot the entire family of power functions y = xn and their mirror images y = x1/n for n = 1 to 100 on the same unit square, and an onion appears. The curves layer over each other from the diagonal outward, peeling apart like the rings of a cross-section.

Each curve y = xn lives between y = x (diagonal, n = 1) and the two axes (n → ∞). Its mirror y = x1/n reflects across the diagonal. Together they fill the unit square like concentric onion skins.

The mathematical idea

For any real exponent n ≥ 1, the curve y = xn on [0, 1] starts at the origin, ends at (1, 1), and bows toward the x-axis. When n = 1 the curve is the diagonal itself. As n grows the curve hugs the x-axis more tightly, until in the limit it collapses onto the axes and the single point (1, 1).

The mirror curve y = x1/n is the inverse: it bows upward toward the y-axis by the same amount, because swapping x and y gives the same graph reflected across y = x. Plotting both families together fills the square symmetrically, and the result looks like the cross-section of an onion: thin outer skins near the diagonal, thicker inner rings as n grows, converging at the center point.

Why n matters

Small values of n produce curves that are hard to tell apart from the diagonal. The visible density is low near n = 1 because all the curves start close together there. As n increases the curves spread out, occupying more of the area between the diagonal and the axes, and the overall shape becomes clearly layered.

Picking a fixed n and tracing it through both families shows a pair of arcs symmetric about y = x. They meet at (0, 0) and (1, 1) and enclose a lens-shaped region whose width grows with n. The visualization lets you hover any curve and read off exactly which n it represents, making the abstract parameter concrete.
